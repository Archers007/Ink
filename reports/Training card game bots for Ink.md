# Plan whole turns, then learn values

Strong trading-card-game bots built without a data centre share one design: they search over the agent's **whole own turn**, score the resulting positions with a **good evaluation function**, and then replace that hand-tuned evaluation with a small value network trained on games played by the search itself, regenerating the data each time the player improves. Ink's hard bot has the right skeleton (determinized Monte Carlo is a sound base for this class of game) but searches only the first atomic action of a 5–15 action turn, hands the rest of the turn and the whole opponent turn to a fixed script, and scores the result with an evaluation that sees no card identities or keywords, plus a 21-feature logistic model whose labels describe who wins when two scripts play each other. Measurements on the real engine in this session fit that reading: the search beats the script 24 of 30 games, but handing it every hidden card won 15 of 30 against the stock bot, lengthening the scripted rollout won 15 of 30, and four times the thinking time won 12 of 20. Those samples only detect effects of roughly 18 points or more, so they rule out hidden information and rollout length as large levers; they do not prove them worthless. The same arithmetic condemns the repo's promotion gate: 80 games passing at 60% will usually reject a real 55% improvement. The order of work that follows is: build a paired, sequential test harness first; take the cheap 8x clone speed-up; add clock, exposure and card-value terms plus lethal and defence rules to the evaluation; move from single-action to whole-turn search; then train a card-aware value network in a generate-train-gate loop with PyTorch and ONNX; then a learned move prior; and only last, opponent-hand inference. End-to-end reinforcement learning, equilibrium solvers, LLM players and an engine port are the wrong investments for one home PC.

## At one-PC scale, strong card bots are turn planners with good evaluators

A decade of competition results points the same way. In the Hearthstone AI Competition the organisers' own summary of winning entries is search "usually paired with a state evaluation function", and in 2020 **all top three agents in both tracks were "dynamic lookahead" planners** over the agent's own turn, winning 72.3%, 70.9% and 68.7% in the premade-deck track ([2020 results](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2020-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf)). In Legends of Code and Magic, search agents "won all competitions running version 1.2"; every one of them pruned moves, most detected lethal, and the reviewers judged that searching the opponent's turn is beneficial but "requires further pruning and heuristic evaluation to make it feasible" ([Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)). In Magic: The Gathering, restructuring the "which cards do I play" choice as a binary tree and pruning dominated moves lifted a determinized search from 43.9% to 50.5% against an expert-rules player while cutting time per move from 0.79 s to 0.20 s; without that restructuring, ten times more simulations made the bot worse (37.3%), because the extra budget went "chasing somewhat arbitrary decision possibilities" ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)). The lesson for a bot that picks one atomic action at a time is direct: structure the turn before buying more samples.

Evaluation quality has repeatedly been worth more than simulation count. An evaluation function with evolved weights and nothing but a greedy one-step lookahead finished **second of 33** in the 2018 Hearthstone competition, ahead of most tree-search entries ([García-Sánchez et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19681)). The competition review states the reason flatly: "full rollouts are too noisy", so search is combined with state evaluation ([Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)). The closest published analogue to Ink's time budget is a Hearthstone MCTS at 0.5 s per move, where adding a three-layer value network (256-128-64 units) as an early-termination evaluator raised the win rate against the plain search from 73.5% to 82.0% in the first seat and from roughly 26.5% to 50.2% in the second; the draft's table labelling is ambiguous, so the seat-by-seat figures carry that caveat, but the direction and size are clear ([Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)). Rollout policy matters in a specific way as well: in Magic, a fully deterministic expert script in the rollouts was "clearly inferior", by around 10 points, to a weaker script that kept some randomness ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)), and a learned card-play policy in the rollouts won 57.2% against the same search without it ([Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)).

Learned policies without search do beat strong humans, but only above a compute threshold a home PC cannot reach. ByteRL's Hearthstone agent trained for **23 days on 24 V100 GPUs and 5,856 CPU cores** ([Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05197)); the released Yu-Gi-Oh agent used over 100 million games, and a three-minute single-GPU run that beat random 98% of the time won 3.1% against it ([ygo-agent](https://github.com/sbl1996/ygo-agent)). Below that threshold, imitation alone disappoints: a network cloned from a tournament-winning MCTS bot plateaued at 59% move agreement and won only 12–15% against strong search bots, because the teacher relies on search information the student never sees ([Loós 2026](https://projekter.aau.dk/learning-based-decision-making-in-a-competitive-card-game-ca046171.html)), and a supervised Hearthstone policy that beat a one-turn search 54.6% of the time won 18.8% against a three-turn search ([Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)). The supported role for a small learned policy is inside the search, as move ordering, prior and rollout guide.

Three fashionable alternatives do not fit. Equilibrium methods of the ReBeL family need to enumerate every private state consistent with public information, which their authors name as the limiting factor ([Brown et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)) and which a later analysis calls "not tractable" for collectible card games ([Haluška & Schmid 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16689)). LLMs as per-move players lose to ordinary search: direct-prompted GPT-4o won 60%, 24% and 12% against tree-search Hearthstone AIs of increasing strength ([Xia et al. 2025](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2025/pdfs/p2795.pdf)), and in the 2025 PokéAgent Challenge "the top participants all used RL or MCTS rather than LLM reasoning", with LLM agents costing $0.014 to $1.25 per game ([PokéAgent Challenge](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15563)). Determinization itself, by contrast, is a defensible base: it performs well where sibling outcomes are correlated across hidden worlds and information is revealed quickly ([Long et al. 2010](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/7562/7562-13-11092-1-2-20201228.pdf)), and the Magic study argues card games fit that description because cards are revealed as they are played ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)). No paper measures those properties for Lorcana, so placing it in the friendly region is an inference.

There is no Lorcana prior art to copy or beat. A search of public repositories found only small partial engines with single-digit stars and no documented method or strength result ([GitHub search](https://github.com/search?q=lorcana&type=repositories&s=stars)), and no paper on Lorcana game-playing AI. Ink's engine, which runs all 2,680 cards, is the unusual asset here, and it means strength has to be measured internally.

## Ink's bot searches one action and scores it blind

The code reading in this session is unambiguous about structure ([diagnosis notes](../research_notes/Training%20card%20game%20bots%20for%20Ink/current_bot_diagnosis.md)). `game/searchbot.js` enumerates the legal single actions at the root (5.5 on average), and for each sample clones the state, re-deals hidden cards uniformly from the opponent's known decklist, applies one candidate, and lets the scripted bot in `game/bot.js` finish the turn and play the opponent's whole turn before a hand-written evaluation scores the position. There is no tree below the root. The search therefore answers "which first action is best if a script finishes my turn", and it re-asks that question from scratch about 42 times a game. At 450 ms it gets about 240 samples per decision, or **about 44 per candidate move**. The evaluation is lore, a per-character term of `2 + 3·lore + 0.45·(strength + willpower)`, hand size and ink terms; Evasive, Ward, Bodyguard, Rush, Singer, Shift and every card ability are invisible to it. The "model" in `game/learn/` is a logistic regression over 21 aggregate features, trained on turn-start positions labelled by eventual winner, where the self-play half of the data is the scripted bot playing itself.

Five matches on the 12 meta decks, with seats and decks alternated and seeds paired, tested which of these limits bind. Four matches shared four vCPUs with other benchmarks, so time-budgeted bots received somewhat fewer samples than on an idle machine, and every interval is a normal approximation on a small sample.

| Candidate | Opponent | Games | Candidate wins | Rate (95% interval) | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Search, 450 ms | Scripted bot | 30 | 24 | 80% ± 14 | The search framework is worth keeping |
| Search, 480 ms | Search, 60 ms | 30 | 19 | 63% ± 17 | 8x time: a small gain, borderline at this sample |
| Search, 1,800 ms | Search, 450 ms | 20 | 12 | 60% ± 21 | 4x time: not distinguishable from even |
| Search that sees all hidden cards | Stock search | 30 | 15 | 50% ± 18 | No measurable edge from perfect information |
| Search with rollouts one turn cycle longer | Stock search | 30 | 15 | 50% ± 18 | No measurable gain from deeper scripted look-ahead |

Read with the caveats, the table says three things. First, **hidden information is not the first problem**: a bot given the opponent's true hand and both deck orders could not beat the bot that guesses. That matches the literature, where an open-hand search showed no significant advantage in 3,562 of 5,000 Dou Di Zhu deals ([Cowling, Powley & Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)) and seeing the opponent's deck was worth 55.5% to a far stronger Hearthstone agent ([Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05197)). A bot that plans one scripted turn ahead with an identity-blind evaluation has no way to use the extra knowledge. Second, more plies of the same script add noise as fast as they add information. Third, thinking time is a weak lever, though this is the least settled of the three: converted with the standard formula ([Chess Programming Wiki](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Match_Statistics)), the point estimates of 63% and 60% equal about 90 and 70 Elo, or 30–35 Elo per doubling, against 57 per doubling measured for a healthy Go MCTS ([Chaslot et al. 2008](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/multithreadedMCTS2.pdf)). Both intervals also include no gain at all. The data cannot separate "flat" from "half a healthy slope", which is why re-running budget scaling at proper sample size is the first experiment in the roadmap. The match with longer rollouts stood at 0–6 after six games and ended 15–15, a fair picture of what 30 games can and cannot show.

The evaluation's prices are measurably wrong. A regression over the 294 text-less characters in Ink's own card database gives printed cost ≈ 0.45·strength + 0.46·willpower + 0.74·lore + 0.49·inkable − 1.65 (R² 0.95), so designers price one lore at about **1.65 stat points; the evaluation prices it at about 6.7** (3 against 0.45). The evaluation consequently scores a fragile 1/1 body with 2 lore (8.9) above a 3/4 body with 1 lore (8.15), where the cost model puts them at about 0.7 and 2.8 ink ([Lorcana notes, local regression](../research_notes/Training%20card%20game%20bots%20for%20Ink/lorcana_specific.md)). On-board lore deserves a premium in a race, but one scaled by the chance the character survives to quest. The evaluation also scores a character identically whether ready or exerted, although only exerted characters can be challenged. Strong players teach the opposite of what these weights encode: "quest only when ahead or safe", remaining willpower over printed willpower ([Karsten](https://ultimateguard.com/es/blog/lorcana-beginners-guide-5-tips-become-better-frank-karsten)), and, when far behind in the race, challenge the opponent's quester instead of taking one more lore ([GeekyDomain](https://geekydomain.com/blogs/guides/lorcana-quest-vs-challenge)). The bot is structurally the "always quest with everything" player the guides warn about, and so is the opponent it imagines.

The learned model fails for a reason Expert Iteration makes precise: a value function fitted to game outcomes estimates the value of the policy that generated the games ([Anthony et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)). Labels from scripted self-play answer "who wins if both sides keep playing like the script", and the repo's own comment records the result: a model that "decides that a big hand wins, and stops inking". One measurement hints at how far scripted games sit from real ones: the scripted bot's first seat won 23 of 60 games (38% ± 12), while human data from the Pixelborn era put the coin-flip winner at 53% overall and 60% among top-100 players ([gleetron](https://gleetron.pages.dev/posts/does-disney-lorcana-have-a-coin-flip-problem)). The sample is small and the interval just reaches 50%, but a policy that cannot convert the tempo of going first is a poor source of value labels. Such data still has a use, since a Hearthstone outcome model trained on random-play games lost only about four points of AUC on MCTS games ([Janusz et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.00730)). It makes a starting point. The gain comes from the loop that regenerates data with the improved player, and Ink has no such loop and no policy learning at all.

## An 80-game gate cannot see a 55% improvement

Every later decision depends on telling a better bot from a lucky one, and the current protocol cannot. At 80 games the 95% interval is ±11 points; a bot that truly wins 55% scores 40 or fewer of 80 in about one match in five, and an equal bot reaches 55% just as often. Detecting 55% against 50% takes roughly 620 to 1,080 games at fixed sample size, or about **550 on average with a sequential probability ratio test**, which also finishes in about 200 games when the true rate is 60% ([engineering notes, computed](../research_notes/Training%20card%20game%20bots%20for%20Ink/engineering_evaluation.md)). The practice to copy is chess-engine testing: play each seed twice with seats and decks swapped and score the pair, which Fishtest reports "saves substantial testing resources" ([Fishtest](https://official-stockfish.github.io/docs/fishtest-wiki/Fishtest-Mathematics.html)); stop by SPRT with α = β = 0.05; first verify the harness by testing a bot against itself ([engine testing guide](https://dannyhammer.github.io/engine-testing-guide/print.html)). Informal peeking at a running score every 20 games produces a 21% false-positive rate on equal bots, so the stopping rule has to be the test's own.

The house standard this implies is an SPRT on game pairs with H0 = 50% and H1 = 55% for anything claimed as an improvement, a non-regression variant (45% against 50%) for refactors and speed work, and a cap near 2,000 games. Three engineering details make it trustworthy. The search bot draws determinization shuffles from `Math.random()`, so it needs its own seeded generator before paired seeds are truly common and before any decision can be replayed. Budgets should be fixed sample counts, not milliseconds, so results do not depend on what else the PC is doing; the diagnostic matches above were distorted by exactly that. Results should be reported as the 12 x 12 deck matrix and split by seat as well as pooled, with one or two decklists and a seed range held out from all tuning, because a bot exploiting a fixed pool looks strong and is not: an exploiter that beat ByteRL 90.4% on 32 deck pools managed 54.2% on 1,024 ([Haluška & Schmid 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16689)). A frozen ladder of references (the script, the search at a quarter and at four times the budget, every promoted version) guards against progress that only beats the previous version.

The cost is affordable. A game with both sides searching at 450 ms takes on the order of 40 s per core, so 1,000 games is 11–12 core-hours, about an hour and a half on eight workers; a quarter-budget first stage runs four times faster. A position suite of 100–300 clear-cut cases (lethal available, opponent lethal to stop, a null challenge into high willpower, inking the only on-curve play) is worth keeping as a seconds-long regression guard, with the chess caveat that such suites reveal breakage and missing knowledge but must never be the tuning target ([Chess Programming Wiki](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Special:MobileDiff/1620)).

## Six stages, each gated by a paired test

The stages are ordered by evidence per unit of effort, and so that each one supplies what the next needs: the harness measures everything, the evaluation is what turn search optimises, the turn-search bot is the teacher for the value network, and the search's visit counts are the targets for the policy.

| Stage | Work | Main evidence | How to know it worked |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Paired SPRT harness, seeded bot RNG, fixed-sample budgets, reference ladder; 8x cheaper clone | 80 games gives ±11 points; clone 570 µs to 69 µs measured | A-vs-A passes about 5% of the time; budget-scaling slope known |
| 1 | Evaluation terms (clocks, exposure, remaining willpower, card value), lethal and defence rules, randomised rollout script, tuned weights | Evolved evaluation plus greedy lookahead placed 2nd of 33 | SPRT pass against stock bot; position suite clean |
| 2 | Whole-turn plan search with pruning and canonical ordering; a few sampled opponent replies per end-of-turn state | Dynamic-lookahead planners swept the 2020 Hearthstone podium | SPRT pass against Stage 1; budget scaling steepens |
| 3 | Card-aware value network trained on search self-play in a generate-train-gate loop (PyTorch to ONNX) | Small value net took a 0.5 s MCTS from about 26.5% to 50.2% in the second seat | SPRT pass per promotion; calibration curve on held-out and human games |
| 4 | Move prior trained on root visit distributions, used for ordering, rollouts and the opponent model | Visit-distribution targets worth about 50 Elo over chosen-move targets | SPRT pass; equal strength at lower budget |
| 5 | Exact card counting, then likelihood-weighted opponent hands | Skat inference gains; one card-game entry got weaker doing it | Open-hand ablation shows an edge first |

### Stage 0: make progress visible, and take the free speed

Alongside the harness, one performance change is too cheap to defer. **56% of the state's bytes are immutable card definitions** that `structuredClone` copies on every sample; sharing `defs` by reference, dropping the log, and using a hand-written clone cut clone time from about 570 µs to 69 µs in this session's benchmark, an 8.3x reduction with no engine rewrite ([engineering notes, measured](../research_notes/Training%20card%20game%20bots%20for%20Ink/engineering_evaluation.md)). One caveat: `s.defs` is assigned only at game setup (`game/engine.js:455`) and read through `D()` everywhere else, but this session did not audit every caller for in-place edits to a definition object, so the shared-`defs` clone should go through the full engine suites and a replay-equality check before it is trusted. A clone currently costs as much as about five engine actions, so this matters most for the arena and data-generation throughput that every later stage consumes. Running independent samples across a worker pool is the natural parallel design, since root parallelization reached a strength-speedup of 14.9 at 16 threads against 8.5 for tree parallelization ([Chaslot et al. 2008](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/multithreadedMCTS2.pdf)), and the root-parallel, hand-evaluated Foul Play bot won its 2025 competition track ([Foul Play](https://pmariglia.github.io/posts/foul-play)). Whether to spend live-play cores that way depends on the first experiment the harness should run: budget scaling at 0.25x, 1x, 4x and 16x with several hundred games per pairing. A flat curve says speed buys nothing yet; a steep one says it does. A Rust or WASM port is not on the list, because card behaviour lives in JavaScript procs and the boundary crossings would consume the gain.

### Stage 1: give the evaluation Lorcana's vocabulary

This is days of hand-coding that every later stage inherits. The terms the strategy literature and the local regression support are turns-to-20 for both players in place of raw lore, an exposure penalty for exerted characters the opponent can profitably challenge next turn, remaining willpower in place of printed willpower, and a per-card value in ink-equivalents from the stat-budget formula with keyword premiums (Evasive about +0.7 ink; Rush, Ward and each point of Resist about +0.5 in the local fit) as starting priors. Hard rules belong beside it: take lethal lore when it exists, and when the opponent threatens 20 next turn, prioritise removing their questers. A Hearthstone "board solver" built from exactly those rules plus greedy trade scoring was a measurable gain over plain MCTS at 0.5 s ([Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)). The rollout script should gain the same rules and some randomness, per the Magic result that deterministic expert rollouts underperform. Weights should then be tuned by evolutionary or Bayesian optimisation against the arena, as the competition winners did ([Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)), not by hand. The existing logistic model should stay switched off until Stage 3 replaces it.

### Stage 2: search the turn, not the first action

The bot should build a full plan for its turn, execute it, and re-plan only when new information arrives (a draw effect, a reveal). Within its own turn the bot knows its hand and board exactly, so the plan search can be shared across all determinizations and needs sampling only to score end-of-turn states against a few opponent replies. A beam or best-first search over action sequences needs the pruning that every competition winner used: a canonical order for independent actions so permutations collapse, removal of dominated moves such as a challenge the defender survives with nothing to follow, and the binary "play this card or not" factoring that made the Magic search both stronger and four times faster. The time budget becomes more generous under this design: at about 42 decisions per game across roughly ten own turns, the bot already spends close to two seconds per turn in aggregate, and a planner spends that on one search instead of four disconnected ones. That figure is arithmetic from the session's measurements, not a benchmark. No source quantifies the gain from turn planning in a lore-race game where only exerted characters can be attacked, so the size of this step for Lorcana is unknown until the SPRT reports it; the direction is the best-evidenced in the literature.

### Stage 3: train a value network on the search's own games

With a competent turn planner as teacher, the proven recipe is Świechowski's: outcome labels, a small multilayer perceptron, positions subsampled within each game because consecutive states are correlated, a rolling buffer of about a million positions, and dozens of iterations of a few thousand search-versus-search games each, 212,000 games in total ([Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)). Undiscounted game outcome is the safe target; replacing it with bootstrapped targets brought "no clear benefit" in a comparable card game ([Zha et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06135)). Inputs must be card-aware and should describe cards by attributes, not ids: 12 decklists exercise only a fraction of the 2,680 cards, and agents built on pure id embeddings are documented as tied to their training deck ([pkm-cabt-ppo](https://huggingface.co/TomatoCream/pkm-cabt-ppo)). A per-card vector of cost, inkable, strength, remaining willpower, lore, type, keywords and ability-kind counts, passed through a shared encoder and pooled per zone, follows the ByteRL pattern of a shared card encoder with mean-pooled zones ([Xi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04096)). Predicting final lore difference as an auxiliary target is cheap and follows KataGo, where dropping such targets caused "a noticeable drop in learning efficiency" ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)).

The pipeline should be offline and file-based, as KataGo runs it with a non-Python engine: self-play workers write data, a separate trainer consumes it, and a gatekeeper match decides promotion ([KataGo](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/SelfplayTraining.md)). For Ink that means Node workers appending float32 feature rows to shard files, PyTorch training on the PC's GPU, export to ONNX, and the Stage 0 SPRT as gatekeeper. Feature extraction must live only in JavaScript, with a schema version checked on both sides. Inference is not a constraint: `onnxruntime-node` evaluated a 41,000-parameter network in **21 µs** per call in this session, against 55 µs for a plain-JavaScript forward pass and roughly 130 µs for one engine action, so no GPU, batching queue or virtual loss is needed in production; TensorFlow.js has had no release since October 2024 ([engineering notes, measured](../research_notes/Training%20card%20game%20bots%20for%20Ink/engineering_evaluation.md); [npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/@tensorflow%2ftfjs-node)). Data is the scarce resource. At about 40 s per game per core with both sides at 450 ms, eight cores produce roughly 17,000 games a day, so the full recipe is about twelve days at lobby budget and about three at a quarter budget; these are estimates from the session's measurements. Generating at reduced budget is reasonable, since the 60 ms bot still won 11 of 30 against the 480 ms bot. The few thousand human games on the site are two to three orders of magnitude too few to train on; their best use is a held-out calibration check that a predicted 60% wins about 60% on human-style positions.

### Stage 4: add a learned move prior

Once the loop runs, the search's root visit distribution is a free policy label. Training on that distribution instead of the single chosen move produced a network about 50 ± 13 Elo stronger at near-identical prediction error ([Anthony et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)). Because Ink's engine already enumerates legal actions, the fitting form is to encode the state once and score each legal action with a shared head, as DouZero does for 27,472 possible moves ([Zha et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06135)). The prior orders the beam, replaces the script in rollouts, and models the opponent, which finally retires the assumption that the opponent quests with everything. KataGo's playout-cap randomisation, a full search on a quarter of decisions and a fast one elsewhere, raises data throughput without degrading policy targets ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)). The cloned policy should not be expected to play well alone.

### Stage 5: infer the opponent's hand, when the bot can use it

Exact card counting against the known decklist is free and should be verified now. Beyond that, the evidence splits: learned inference lifted an expert-level Skat program by more than four tournament points per game ([Solinas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09604)), while a card-game entry that added hand prediction to a winning search "reduced the overall win rate, most likely due to less time available for search" ([Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)), and no study quantifies hand inference with a known decklist in a collectible card game. The session's open-hand ablation is the right trigger: repeat it after each stage, and build inference when the clairvoyant bot starts winning by a detectable margin. The cheap form weights sampled hands by a likelihood computed once per decision from what the opponent inked and declined to play.

## Conclusion

The session's most reusable result is an instrument more than a number. The open-hand ablation and the budget-scaling match are upper-bound experiments: each asks what a perfect version of one component would be worth before anyone builds it. Today both return little, which says the bot's ceiling is set by what it knows about Lorcana and how much of a turn it considers, with neither hidden cards nor raw compute as the binding limit. As the evaluation and the turn search improve, both instruments should start returning more, and that change is the signal for when parallel workers and hand inference become worth their cost. A roadmap ordered this way corrects itself as the bot changes, where a fixed list of techniques would not.

Two consequences reach beyond the opponent bot. A calibrated value in win-probability units is what makes a chess.com-style review honest, since that site classifies moves by expected points lost at thresholds of 5, 10 and 20 points ([chess.com](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8572705-how-are-moves-classified-what-is-a-blunder-or-brilliant-etc)); Ink's search already keeps per-move sample variance, so the coach can label a move only when the gap exceeds its own noise and say "close call" otherwise. And because no public Lorcana engine or dataset comes close to a complete, deterministic, replayable 2,680-card simulator, the self-play corpus this roadmap produces would be the first of its kind. What remains uncertain is the size of each step: every effect size cited here comes from Hearthstone, Magic, Legends of Code and Magic or classical card games, and Lorcana's exert-to-be-attacked rule and lore race have no published AI results. The harness in Stage 0 is what turns those borrowed expectations into measurements.
