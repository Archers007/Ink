# Search and planning algorithms for imperfect-information, stochastic, multi-action-turn card games (Hearthstone / MTG / Lorcana class)

Reading notes for the report writer:

- Evidence labels used below: **[PR]** = peer-reviewed paper or journal; **[PP]** = arXiv preprint (not confirmed peer-reviewed); **[COMP]** = competition organisers' result slides; **[GREY]** = GitHub README, wiki, model card, blog.
- "Read in full text" means the numbers were taken from the paper's own text/tables (PDF extracted locally). "Abstract/snippet only" means only the abstract or a search summary was available; treat those as weaker.
- Target constraints assumed throughout: Node.js engine, about 7,500 engine actions/second/core, 450 ms to about 1 s per decision, 4–16 cores, optional single consumer GPU. Current bot = flat root-only determinized Monte Carlo with UCB1 over single atomic actions, scripted playouts to own next turn, hand-tuned evaluation.

## 1. Perfect Information Monte Carlo (PIMC / determinization): why it works, how it fails, and when the failures matter

### Takeaway
PIMC works well when (a) the values of alternative moves are highly correlated across hidden worlds and (b) hidden information is revealed quickly as play proceeds; trick-taking games sit in that region, poker does not. Its two structural errors (strategy fusion, non-locality) are not removed by more samples, and its third cost is budget splitting across trees; for a CCG the measured losses from determinization are small relative to the gains from better evaluation, pruning and rollouts.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR, read in full text]** Long, Sturtevant, Buro, Furtak (AAAI 2010) define the two PIMC errors after Frank & Basin (1998): *strategy fusion* (the search assumes it can pick a different move in each sampled world although it cannot tell those worlds apart) and *non-locality* (the value of a node depends on parts of the tree outside its subtree, because an opponent with private knowledge steers play toward worlds good for them). — [Long et al. 2010, AAAI](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/7562/7562-13-11092-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- **[PR]** The paper explains PIMC's practical success with three measurable tree properties: *leaf correlation* (probability that sibling terminal values agree), *bias* (how often the game favours one player), and *disambiguation factor* (how fast information sets shrink with each move). — [Long et al. 2010](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7562)
- **[PR]** On synthetic trees, "PIMC search is at its worst when leaf node correlation is low", both in absolute terms and relative to random play; bias has a "generally small" effect; as disambiguation approaches 1, PIMC "improves drastically, since the game is approaching a perfect information game". — [Long et al. 2010](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/7562/7562-13-11092-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- **[PR]** Measured on real games: Skat and Hearts show leaf correlation from 0.8 to nearly 1.0 and disambiguation about 0.6, a region where PIMC loses only about 0.1 points per game (payoffs in [-1, 1]) to an equilibrium player and gains about 0.4 over random. Kuhn poker has disambiguation 0 and correlation 0.5, the region where PIMC is predicted to be poor; against a best-response opponent PIMC loses 0.083 (as player 1) and 0.166 (as player 2) per game, versus a Nash value of ±1/18. The authors give this as one explanation of why poker research went to equilibrium-finding on abstractions rather than PIMC. — [Long et al. 2010](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/7562/7562-13-11092-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- **[PR]** PIMC had already produced expert-level programs in Bridge (Ginsberg's GIB) and Skat (Buro et al. 2009) and strong play in Hearts, despite textbook criticism of "averaging over clairvoyance". — [Long et al. 2010](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7562)
- **[PR, read in full text]** Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse (IEEE TCIAIG 2012) add a third shortcoming of determinization: "the computational budget must be shared between searching several independent trees rather than devoting all iterations to exploring deeply a single tree". — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** How much hidden information is actually worth, measured by "cheating" UCT that sees all cards: in Dou Di Zhu the Landlord win rate was 43.6% for determinized UCT, 42.3% for ISMCTS and 56.5% for cheating UCT. Across 5,000 deals, cheating UCT significantly outperformed determinized UCT in 1,421 and there was no significant difference in 3,562; the authors call it "an unusual feature of Dou Di Zhu among hidden information games that having access to hidden information only provides a strong advantage in a minority of deals", and conclude strategy fusion "is not a major factor in this game". — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** In Magic: The Gathering, a single determinized UCT tree with 10,000 simulations won only 26–32% against rule-based players; splitting the same 10,000 simulations over 40 determinizations (250 each) raised this to 47% against the expert-rules player and 56% against the weaker reduced-rules player. The best number of determinizations was 20–100, and with a 100,000-simulation budget the best simulations-per-determinization was still 100–1,000, "suggesting that an increased simulation budget is best used in running additional determinizations rather than searching each determinization more deeply". At the other extreme, 1,000 trees of 10 simulations collapsed to 26%/41%. — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR]** The same MTG paper argues the game suits Monte Carlo methods because of "the progressive revealing of information as players draw new cards from their decks and play them out combined with the relative unlikelihood of similar game states leading to radically different game outcomes". — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR]** More samples do not fix strategy fusion. In an AlphaZero-with-PIMC system for Barrage Stratego, 3 sampled worlds per move outperformed 6 or 12, and the authors state "we do not solve the problem of strategy fusion by using more samples even with infinite samples". — [Blüml, Czech, Kersting 2023, Frontiers in AI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10213697/)
- **[PR survey]** The 2023 MCTS survey lists the same three determinization problems (strategy fusion, non-locality, budget split across trees) and cites the MTG work as the card-game application, noting it needed domain pruning and a binary tree to control branching. — [Świechowski, Godlewski, Sawicki, Mańdziuk 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04931)

### Inferences
- Lorcana resembles the trick-taking/MTG region more than the poker region. Cards are revealed when played (high disambiguation), there is no betting or bluffing action whose only purpose is to signal, and whether a board position is good is mostly the same across plausible opponent hands (high leaf correlation). By the Long et al. framework PIMC should be a reasonable base, and the dominant errors in the current bot are more likely the scripted opponent model and the single-action horizon than determinization itself.
- Strategy fusion does bite in one CCG-specific place: decisions that are only good if you later get to react with knowledge you will not have (e.g. holding back a removal or a singer "because the search will know what the opponent holds next turn"). A flat root search that re-deals every sample is immune to *deep* strategy fusion at its own later decisions only because it does not search them at all; once the search is deepened into the opponent's turn with per-world trees, fusion appears at opponent nodes (the opponent is modelled as knowing our hand).
- The MTG result (40 × 250 beats 1 × 10,000 and 1,000 × 10) implies a practical rule: several dozen determinizations, each searched to a useful depth. The current bot's "new deal every sample" is the 1,000 × 10 end of that spectrum when combined with any deeper tree, but is fine for a flat root search where statistics are pooled at the root.
- Budget arithmetic for the target: 450 ms × 7,500 actions/s ≈ 3,400 engine actions per decision per core. A playout through the rest of the turn plus the opponent's turn is on the order of 20–30 actions, so roughly 100–170 playouts per decision per core. Published determinized-MCTS results use 10,000+ simulations (Cowling) or 5–10 s per move (Zhang & Buro). The Ink budget is one to two orders of magnitude smaller, so sample efficiency (pruning, short playouts, a good evaluation) matters more than in the papers.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that measures leaf correlation / disambiguation for Hearthstone, MTG or Lorcana directly; the "CCGs are in the PIMC-friendly region" statement is an inference.
- Frank & Basin (1998) was not read directly; its definitions are taken from Long et al. and Cowling et al.
- No measurement was found of how much a "cheating" (open-hand) agent gains in Hearthstone or MTG specifically, other than the indirect Dockhorn result in section 5.

## 2. Information Set MCTS, open-loop MCTS and chance-node handling, compared with flat PIMC

### Takeaway
ISMCTS (one tree over the searcher's information sets, a fresh determinization per iteration) beats separate determinized trees when deep search pays or strategy fusion is severe, and only ties when the opponent branching factor is huge; in MTG and Hearthstone the published systems that worked were determinized ensembles or hybrids with heavy pruning, open-loop statistics, and chance-node sampling or bucketing. Explicit chance nodes for every draw were clearly worse.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR, read in full text]** Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse (2012) introduce three variants: SO-ISMCTS (single observer; one tree of the root player's information sets, opponent moves treated as fully observable), SO-ISMCTS+POM (partially observable moves), and MO-ISMCTS (multiple observer; one tree per player's viewpoint). Each iteration samples one determinization and restricts selection to actions legal in it, using a UCB variant that counts how often an action was *available*. — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** Results by domain: in Lord of the Rings: The Confrontation "a deep search is required for strong play, and so ISMCTS has the advantage"; in the Phantom (4,4,4) game strategy fusion is "particularly detrimental, so again ISMCTS outperforms determinization"; in Dou Di Zhu "the information set tree has an orders-of-magnitude larger branching factor than a determinization tree, so the two algorithms are on a par" (43.6% vs 42.3% as Landlord). — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** Cost: "in 1 s of decision time SO-ISMCTS and MO-ISMCTS execute around a third the number of iterations that determinized UCT does" (Dou Di Zhu and LOTR:C). Even so, with enough decision time MO-ISMCTS showed "a clear advantage over determinized UCT, which increases with increasing CPU time" in LOTR:C. — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** The authors' summary: "In domains where deep search is possible and beneficial or strategy fusion is detrimental, ISMCTS shows great promise. However, in domains such as Dou Di Zhu, where information sets have large numbers of legal moves and the effect of strategy fusion is not so clear, ISMCTS offers no immediate benefit over existing approaches." They also note ISMCTS still does not address non-locality and that SO-ISMCTS assumes a cheating opponent. — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** The best determinization/iteration split is game-dependent: Dou Di Zhu was insensitive as long as both numbers were large enough (40 × 250 used), while in LOTR:C strength worsened as the number of determinizations rose at a fixed total (a Light-side 1 × 10,000 player "significantly outperforms a 40 × 250 player by 22.9%"), which the authors attribute to more iterations per tree rather than fewer trees. In the same game the gap between determinized UCT and MO-ISMCTS for the Light player was about 4.4% (significant at 95%). — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR, read in full text]** MTG: MCTS with explicit chance nodes for every card draw ("All Possible Deals") won only 23% against the expert-rules player and 38% against the reduced-rules player; the tree "grows so rapidly with depth" because each draw has a branching factor of 15 or more. Ensemble determinization (40 trees × 250) reached 47%/56% in the same setting. — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR]** MTG enhancement study (win % vs the expert-rules player, 10,000 simulations): no enhancements 43.9% at 0.79 s/move; dominated-move pruning (MP) alone 46.1%; binary yes/no decision tree (BT) alone 47.6% at 0.19 s/move; BT + MP 50.5% at 0.20 s/move. With 100,000 rollouts: no enhancements 37.3% (worse than with 10,000), BT 52.0%. The authors attribute the regression without BT to the large branching factor: extra budget "is used in chasing somewhat arbitrary decision possibilities". — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR]** Context for those percentages: the expert-rules MTG player beat the reduced-rules player 63.7% (±0.94%) and won 42.1% of 114 games against two strong human players (46.6% of 58 and 37.5% of 56), so a search agent at about 50% against it is around competent-human level in that restricted card pool. — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR, read in full text]** Hearthstone, Zhang & Buro (CIG 2017): determinized UCT ("DUCT") over a fixed set of pre-sampled worlds (numWorlds = 10), with statistics for an action shared between the worlds in which it is legal; chance nodes are sampled on traversal; rollouts are depth-limited (d = 5) and end in Silverfish's hand-tuned end-of-turn evaluation. With 5 s per move DUCT beat the expert-tuned Silverfish 3-ply expectimax 60.1% (SE 2.0) over three mirror matchups. — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PR]** Chance-node bucketing (Zhang & Buro): group possible draws into M buckets by mana cost (e.g. Mech Mage: [1] [2] [3] [4,5] [6..10]) and pre-sample N outcomes per bucket; best was N = 2 when one card is drawn and N = 1 when more are drawn. This raised the win rate against Silverfish from 60.1% to 72.3% (SE 1.8). — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PP → CIG 2018, read via ar5iv]** Hearthstone, Świechowski, Tajmajer, Janusz: a PIMC/ISMCTS hybrid. The hidden state is re-sampled at the start of each iteration; information sets are used as keys in a transposition table; chance nodes are removed entirely, the successor state is recomputed each time a move is replayed, and move statistics are averaged over the random outcomes (i.e. open-loop statistics). — [Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)
- **[PR, abstract only]** Choe & Kim (IEEE CoG 2019) fold equivalent Hearthstone states into a directed acyclic graph by state abstraction, run UCT on the graph, and use sparse sampling to handle imperfect information and randomness, which also cuts the stochastic branching factor. — [Choe & Kim 2019](https://pure.korea.ac.kr/en/publications/enhancing-monte-carlo-tree-search-for-playing-hearthstone/)
- **[PR, competition summary]** Kowalski & Miernik's review states that most CCG-playing work "took the form of MCTS enhancements", that "the algorithm seems to work well in such a stochastic, multi-action environment", and that "full rollouts are too noisy. Thus, search is usually combined with some form of state evaluation based on expert knowledge and heuristics or neural networks". — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023, IEEE CoG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PP]** ByteDance's LOCM paper calls IS-MCTS "game theoretically 'unsafe' as it neglects the entering probability at an information state due to the opponent policy", while acknowledging "it indeed shows good performance on some card games". — [Xi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04096)
- **[search snippet only, not verified in the source text]** Later Dou Di Zhu work is reported to cite Furtak & Buro (2013) for an "information leaking" problem in ISMCTS. — [DeltaDou, IJCAI 2019 (candidate citing paper)](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2019/176)

### Inferences
- For Lorcana the opponent's branching factor at the information-set level is large (any card of a 60-card list could be in hand), which is the Dou Di Zhu condition under which ISMCTS only tied determinized UCT while running at a third of the speed. The more useful ideas to borrow from ISMCTS are (1) one shared tree across determinizations for *our own* decisions within the current turn, where our information set is fully known and strategy fusion is nil, and (2) availability-count UCB.
- The consistent finding across MTG and Hearthstone is that explicit chance branching is harmful and that sampling outcomes (open-loop statistics or a handful of pre-sampled/bucketed outcomes) is the workable treatment. For Lorcana's main chance event (one draw per turn, plus occasional draw/shuffle effects) open-loop averaging at the action level is the simplest match.
- The MTG "more simulations made the un-pruned player worse" result is directly relevant: adding cores or time to the present bot without restructuring the action space may not help.

### Gaps
- Exact win rates for Choe & Kim (2019) and for Santos, Santos & Melo's "Monte Carlo tree search experiments in Hearthstone" (CIG 2017; heuristic-guided selection/simulation plus a deck database, reported as beating vanilla MCTS and Metastone's built-in AI) were not retrievable; only abstracts/summaries were read. — [Santos et al. project page](https://amlsantos.github.io/blog/mcts.html)
- No head-to-head of SO-ISMCTS vs ensemble PIMC vs flat Monte Carlo was found for Hearthstone or MTG under an identical budget.
- The Whitehouse/Powley/Cowling 2011 Dou Di Zhu paper and Furtak & Buro 2013 were not read directly.

## 3. The long-turn problem: planning a 5–15 action turn, and what the Hearthstone AI Competition and LOCM winners did

### Takeaway
The dominant, repeatedly winning recipe in CCG competitions is not deep game-tree search: it is a search over *the agent's own turn* (best-first/beam/"dynamic lookahead", pruned BFS, depth-limited MCTS or evolutionary planning over action sequences) that scores end-of-turn states with a tuned evaluation function, plus lethal detection and aggressive move ordering/pruning; looking into the opponent's reply helps but needs still more pruning. Factoring the action space (binary decisions, action types, high-level/low-level split) is the best-evidenced way to make tree search over a turn tractable.

### Cited Findings
**Hearthstone AI Competition (SabberStone, C#, partial observation; 2018–2020)**
- **[PR summary]** Time budget was 30 seconds per turn. "Winning strategies of the submitted agents were mostly based on search algorithms: Rolling Horizon Evolution, MCTS, Pruned BFS, or Dynamic Lookahead algorithm; usually paired with a state evaluation function." The best agent of 2019 is described as based on Information Set MCTS and sparse sampling (Choe & Kim); the 2018 runner-up used competitive co-evolution to learn a heuristic evaluation function used in a greedy one-step look-ahead. — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[COMP] 2018** premade-deck track (33 submissions): 1st Max Frick & Ünal Akkaya 76.0% win rate; 2nd "EVA" (Pablo García-Sánchez, Alberto Tonda) 74.2%; 3rd Kai Bornemann 72.5%. Organisers' conclusion: "Top-performing agents either used simulation-based search algorithms, such as MCTS, or trained an evaluation function using evolutionary algorithms." User-created-deck track (17 submissions): 1st Lars Wagner (Jade Druid) 95%; 2nd Frick & Akkaya (Warlock) 88%; "Winning strategies include: MCTS, RHEA, EA Greedy". — [2018 results slides](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2018-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf)
- **[PR]** The 2018 runner-up is documented: evolved weights for a state evaluation used by a greedy one-step look-ahead finished second of 33. — [García-Sánchez et al., "Optimizing Hearthstone Agents using an Evolutionary Algorithm"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19681)
- **[COMP] 2019** premade-deck track (16 submissions) finalists: BotHeimbrodt (pruned BFS + heuristics), Tyche Agent B (MCTS), AlvaroAgent (MCTS), BestChildAgent (pruned BFS); final win rates 57.66%, 55.22%, 50.00%, 37.11% (the slide text extraction does not preserve which agent got which rank). User-created track finalists: TycheAgent (Jade Druid, MCTS), JadeDruidBot (EA), MCGSAgent (Odd Paladin, Monte Carlo graph search; Choe), GreedyEA; rates 79.57%, 77.92%, 76.35%, 64.14%. Organisers: most frequent agent types were "simulation-based search algorithms, such as MCTS" or "trained state evaluation functions using EAs". — [2019 results slides](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2019-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf)
- **[COMP] 2020** premade-deck track (23 submissions, 9 decks of which 3 secret): all top three were "Dynamic Lookahead (DL)" agents: 1st Sebastian Miller 72.34%, 2nd Team CopyCats 70.91%, 3rd GretiveComp 68.71%. The organisers describe the most frequent agent types as MCTS-style simulation search "and modifications of dynamic lookahead (winner of last year)". User-created track (18 submissions): top three also all DL agents (65.47%, 63.23%, 59.76%). — [2020 results slides](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2020-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf)
- Source conflict: Kowalski & Miernik name the ISMCTS + sparse sampling agent as "the best agent of 2019", while the organisers' 2020 slides call dynamic lookahead the "winner of last year". The competition had two tracks, which may reconcile the two statements; the 2019 slides as extracted do not settle it. — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814); [2020 slides](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2020-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf)

**Legends of Code and Magic / Strategy Card Game AI Competition (2018–2022)**
- **[PR]** CodinGame 2018 contest (2,174 marathon submissions): "the best players employed variants of well-known search methods like Minimax (few plys deep; alpha-beta and heuristic pruning) and MCTS (depth-limited with a heuristic cut-off). The most significant improvements reported by all players were move ordering and pruning, and lethal (winning) move detection." — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PR]** IEEE CEC 2019 winner Coac: "minimax-like search of depth three with alpha-beta and heuristic pruning", 94.22% win rate, 33% higher than the runner-up. — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PR]** "Search-based agents were the most common and won all competitions running version 1.2. All of them employed move pruning either explicitly (certain actions were disallowed) or implicitly (moves were generated out of ordered actions). Similarly, most of them implemented lethal move detection. Only some of the agents searched through the opponent's turn, and it is clear that while it is beneficial to do so, it requires further pruning and heuristic evaluation to make it feasible with the exploding branching factor." — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PR]** Agent designs listed there: best-first search limited to own turn with a linear evaluation of hand-made features tuned by Bayesian optimisation (ReinforcedGreediness); "flat simulation-based algorithm, simulating own turn and the opponent's response" (DrainPower, 1st and 2nd at CoG 2021); minimax depth four over own actions and depth one over the opponent's (Fabbiamo); Online Evolutionary Planning where the genome is a series of actions and mutation only reorders them (UJIAgent3); MCTS with opponent-hand prediction (Chad); evaluation weights tuned offline by evolutionary algorithms, harmony search or MCTS over parameter space (Zylo, Chad, AdvancedAvocadoAgent). — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PR]** A cautionary result: ProphetCoac added opponent-hand prediction to the winning minimax agent to cut the branching factor; "ultimately it did not improve but actually reduced the overall win rate, most likely due to less time available for search" (87.84% vs Coac's 89.88% at CoG 2019). — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)

**Published techniques for multi-action turns**
- **[PR]** Zhang & Buro's time management for turn sequences: the turn plan is built by recursively taking the most-visited child within the turn; if a move in the returned sequence has fewer than ψ = 50 visits, a new search is started from the preceding node with the reserved fraction (1 − β) of the time (β = 2/3). — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PR]** Zhang & Buro's high-level/low-level action split: "play card" decisions are high-level and target selection is a dependent low-level decision; "once the high-level actions are fixed, we only need to search the low-level actions". Rollouts sample a card from a learned high-level policy and pick targets with a fast heuristic. — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PR]** Silverfish, the strong pre-MCTS Hearthstone bot they compare with, used an expert-tuned end-of-turn evaluation, a move-pruning system and a 3-turn expectimax with opponent modelling, and was reported to beat rank-10 human players. — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PP → CIG 2018]** Świechowski et al. split compound actions into atomic steps (card, then target, then battlecry target), and add a greedy "board solver" for attack ordering (lethal check first, then threats, then board gain minus loss) exposed to MCTS as a single artificial "use solver" action available once per turn. At 0.5 s/move the solver alone scored 79.0% as first player (plain-MCTS mirror baseline 73.5%) and 46.8% as second player; the value network alone scored higher than the solver in both seats (82.0% and 50.2%). Table labelling in the available draft is ambiguous, so treat the seat-by-seat reading with care. — [Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)
- **[PR]** Cowling, Ward & Powley turned the "which subset of cards do I play" choice into a binary yes/no tree over cards in descending mana order, and pruned dominated moves (e.g. always play a land when able; do not consider a play dominated by a larger one). The binary tree both raised strength and cut time per move about 4× (0.79 s → 0.19 s). — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR, IEEE CoG 2023, partial text]** McKenney & Ruml's "one-ply MCTS for CCGs" for Duelyst II: rollouts stop at the end of the agent's own turn "because the opponent's turn cannot be accurately simulated without a good model of their deck and hand"; rollouts are random or greedy hill-climbing on a static evaluator; actions are factored into a tree of action type → action source → ground action. It consistently beat the game's rule-based starter AI with little domain knowledge and no learning. — [McKenney & Ruml 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10465745)
- **[PR, as described by McKenney & Ruml]** Dockhorn et al.'s Hearthstone agent searched three plies (own turn, opponent turn, own turn): rollouts greedily select actions with a static evaluator and stop at the end of the ply; the n best end-of-turn states become roots for searches of the opponent's ply; the algorithm returns the best *sequence* of actions rather than a single action; opponent deck and hand are predicted from previous plays. — [McKenney & Ruml 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10465745)
- **[PR, abstract only]** Justesen, Mahlmann, Risi, Togelius (IEEE Trans. Games 2018): Online Evolutionary Planning evolves the sequence of actions for a single turn against a state-evaluation fitness; in Hero Academy, constrained MCTS variants beat vanilla MCTS "by a large margin", and OEP searched the plan space more efficiently than any tested tree search, with a relative advantage that grows with the number of actions per turn. The 2016 precursor reported Online Evolution beating standard MCTS and two greedy baselines by a large margin. — [Justesen et al. 2018](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/c9df9472-81f7-4873-b5f4-eba203d44526); [Justesen et al. 2016](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/online-evolution-for-multi-action-adversarial-games/)
- **[PR]** Hoover et al. state the general difficulty: Hearthstone branching can reach 375,000 for a turn in some states, and "it is very hard to use MCTS effectively beyond a single turn given the partial observability of the game". — [Hoover et al. 2019, "The Many AI Challenges of Hearthstone"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06562)

### Inferences
- Across three competition series and a decade of papers, the agent that plans its *whole own turn* against an end-of-turn evaluation beats agents that pick one atomic action at a time. The present Ink bot does the opposite (one action, then script). The lowest-risk upgrade suggested by the evidence is a within-turn search (beam/best-first over action sequences with transposition of permutations, or MCTS with turn-end cut-off) scored by evaluation after a cheap model of the opponent's reply.
- Because Lorcana's own-turn decisions are made under perfect information about our own hand and board (only draw effects and the opponent's reply are uncertain), the within-turn part can be searched as a deterministic/open-loop problem and shared across all determinizations; determinization is only needed to score end-of-turn states.
- Lorcana-specific pruning analogous to the MTG and LOCM rules is likely to pay most: canonical ordering of independent actions (ink before plays, quests that cannot be affected by later actions in a fixed order), dominated-move removal, and a dedicated lethal/"reach 20 lore" check on both sides. The binary "play this card or not" factoring maps naturally onto "ink which card / play which subset under the ink budget".
- The ProphetCoac result warns that features which cost search time (including hand inference) can net out negative under a tight clock; this applies with more force at 450 ms in Node.js.
- With about 3,400 engine actions per core per decision, exhaustive enumeration of a 10-action turn is out of reach, but a beam of a few dozen partial plans to turn end, each scored by a handful of opponent-reply samples, fits the budget.

### Gaps
- The exact definition of "Dynamic Lookahead" was not found in a primary source; from its use alongside "greedy one-step look-ahead" and "pruned BFS" it appears to be a best-first/depth-adaptive lookahead over own-turn action sequences with a tuned evaluation, but that is not confirmed.
- The 2019 Hearthstone AI Competition rank-to-agent mapping could not be recovered from the slides.
- Hero Academy win-rate tables for OEP vs MCTS variants (full text returned HTTP 403) and the Duelyst II result tables (PDF truncated) were not read.
- No published transposition-table/permutation-pruning study specific to CCG turn sequences was found beyond Choe & Kim's state-abstraction DAG (abstract only).

## 4. Learned value functions and policy priors instead of random/scripted rollouts

### Takeaway
Every source that compared them found rollout/evaluation quality worth more than raw simulation count: cutting rollouts short and scoring with a (learned or tuned) value function, and biasing rollouts with a cheap learned or rule-based policy that keeps some randomness, each gave gains of roughly 5–25 win-rate points. Small MLPs on hand-built features were enough; fully deterministic expert playouts were worse than semi-random ones.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR]** Rollout policy in MTG: "Applying a fully deterministic rollout strategy, as we did when using our expert rules player to handle the rollouts, provided a clearly inferior performance to utilising the reduced rules player which uses very limited domain knowledge, but incorporates some randomness within its decisions ... despite the fact that the expert rules player is an intrinsically stronger player." Expert-rules rollouts were worse "by around 10% in most cases"; uniformly random rollouts "proved to be very weak". — [Cowling, Ward, Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)
- **[PR]** Zhang & Buro: depth-limited rollouts (d = 5) ending in an expert evaluation function were the base design. Adding a learned high-level card-play policy to rollouts (plus heuristic target selection) won 57.2% (SD 1.3; 500 games per mirror match, 10 s/move) against the same search without it; per deck 53.4% / 62.6% / 55.6%. The policy rollout was 5–10× slower than plain rollouts and still a net gain. — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PR]** Same paper, policy without search: a CNN card-play policy plus greedy targeting (given open hands) won 99.7% vs random, 71.3% vs the greedy heuristic, 54.6% vs Silverfish 1-turn search but only 18.8% vs Silverfish 3-turn search: "it still cannot beat the search-based 3-turn look ahead Silverfish". The small DNN (230K parameters) took about 60 microseconds per mini-batch evaluation and was chosen for rollouts over CNNs that were 10× slower. — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf)
- **[PP → CIG 2018]** Świechowski, Tajmajer, Janusz: value network = three dense layers (256, 128, 64, tanh) on a 750-element feature vector (low-level features plus 10-dimensional word2vec-style card embeddings). It is used for *early termination*: random simulation stops at the end of a player's turn but not before k = 20 steps, and the tree is updated with the network's win probability. Training data: 3.5M+ samples from open-hand ("cheater") MCTS self-play at 1 s/move over 400 decks (validation accuracy 0.76); then iterative retraining, 20,000 initial games + 3,000 games per iteration, 1M-sample FIFO buffer, 64 iterations. — [Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)
- **[PP → CIG 2018]** Their results (500 games each vs plain MCTS; plain MCTS mirror as first player wins 73.5% at 0.5 s/move and 70.6% at 1 s/move): MCTS + value net as first player 82.0% (0.5 s) and 76.0% (1 s); as second player 50.2% (0.5 s) and 55.9% (1 s), against an implied plain-MCTS second-player baseline of about 26.5% / 29.4%. Value net + solver as second player 56.1% / 55.2%. The abstract's conclusion: small networks guiding search "substantially improve the win rate, while reducing computation". Table labelling in the available draft is ambiguous, so treat exact figures with care. — [Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)
- **[PP → CIG 2018]** Same paper vs humans (1 s/move, value net + solver): about level with regular-rank players (7–7 in one reported row) and below Legend-rank players (a 12–9 row reported as 43% and a 3–15 row as 17%; it is unclear from the draft which side each count refers to). Samples are tens of games. — [Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)
- **[PR]** Competition evidence that a tuned evaluation beats long rollouts: "full rollouts are too noisy. Thus, search is usually combined with some form of state evaluation"; LOCM agents used "MCTS (depth-limited with a heuristic cut-off)"; several winners tuned evaluation weights by evolutionary algorithms or Bayesian optimisation. — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PR]** An evaluation function alone, tuned by co-evolution and used with one-step greedy lookahead, placed 2nd of 33 in the 2018 Hearthstone AI Competition, ahead of most MCTS entries. — [García-Sánchez et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19681)
- **[PR]** AlphaZero-style PUCT with determinization exists as a published baseline: AlphaZe** keeps AlphaZero's network and training loop, runs one PUCT search per sampled world (3 samples for Barrage Stratego, 12 for DarkHex) and averages the resulting *policies* rather than values. With 25,000 nodes per tree it averaged 59.6% against existing Barrage Stratego bots (over 70% excluding P2SRO) but won only 16% against P2SRO; training took about 16 days on 3 V100 GPUs for Stratego and about 26 hours for DarkHex 11×11. — [Blüml, Czech, Kersting 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10213697/)
- **[PP, search snippet only]** A 2026 preprint (MAPLE) reports outperforming AlphaZe** on Phantom Go and Dark Hex by aggregating evaluation over multiple states, citing strategy fusion as the PIMC weakness it targets. — [MAPLE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24139)
- **[PP]** Adding search to training of a pure-policy CCG agent gave little: ByteRL tried IS-MCTS (expanding from the ground-truth state) to generate training targets in LOCM; under an equal resource budget the MCTS variant won 56% vs the model-free baseline at 40 h of training, 52% at 80 h and 51% at 144 h, so "we haven't applied MCTS, because it produces less samples under the same resource budget and its promotion is unsatisfactory". — [Xi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04096)
- **[GREY]** A community Pokémon TCG agent uses a roughly 0.5M-parameter pointer-style policy/value network trained with PPO self-play, with one checkpoint produced by expert iteration that distils determinized IS-MCTS targets into the network; reported evaluation is only against a random agent (80–100% over about 20 games). — [pkm-cabt-ppo model card](https://huggingface.co/TomatoCream/pkm-cabt-ppo)

### Inferences
- The two cheapest, best-evidenced changes for the Ink bot are (1) stop the playout at a turn boundary and evaluate, which it already does, and (2) make the playout policy and the evaluation better. The Świechowski result (first-player win rate 73.5% → 82.0% at 0.5 s; second-player rate rising from an implied 26–29% to 50–56% if the table is read seat-by-seat) is the closest published analogue to Ink's budget and shows a small MLP value function trained on self-play outcomes is worth more than doubling the time (their 0.5 s → 1 s change moved results by only a few points).
- A value network of the Świechowski size (about 250K weights) is a few hundred microseconds or less per evaluation in plain JavaScript typed arrays, i.e. comparable to a handful of engine actions, so replacing the remaining scripted playout (20–30 actions) with one evaluation after a short reply model could multiply the number of samples per decision several-fold. This is an estimate, not a measured figure.
- Training data can be generated on the same PC by open-hand self-play of the existing search bot (the "cheater MCTS" trick); 3.5M samples in the Hearthstone paper corresponds to tens of thousands of games, which at Ink's engine speed is hours to days of 8–16 core time rather than a cluster job. This is an extrapolation.
- The Cowling finding that a deterministic expert playout is worse than a semi-random weak one bears directly on the current design, where "a simple scripted heuristic bot plays both sides": if that script is deterministic, every sample of a given deal returns the same continuation, and the opponent model has no variance. Injecting randomness (softmax over script preferences, or epsilon-random) is supported by that result.
- Zhang & Buro's 18.8% (policy only) vs 3-turn search, and ByteRL's opposite result at vastly larger scale (section 7), suggest that at hobby-scale compute a learned policy is best used as a prior/playout guide inside search rather than as the whole agent.

### Gaps
- No published CCG study was found that holds the value function fixed and varies only simulations (or vice versa) over a wide range; the "evaluation quality beats sample count" conclusion rests on the comparisons above (0.5 s vs 1 s in Świechowski; 10K vs 100K rollouts in Cowling; DL/EA agents beating MCTS agents in competitions).
- No AlphaZero-style (PUCT + policy/value net + determinization) result on Hearthstone, MTG or LOCM with competitive evaluation was found; AlphaZe** is on Stratego/DarkHex.
- No measurements of neural-network inference cost in Node.js for this use were sourced.

## 5. Opponent hand / deck inference and belief-biased determinization

### Takeaway
Predicting the opponent's deck/hand from observed plays is easy to do well in a constructed meta (above 95% next-card accuracy mid-game in Hearthstone from bigram statistics) and measurably improves determinized search when the deck is unknown; when the decklist is already known, as in Ink, the remaining gain comes from inferring what is *in hand* from what the opponent did and did not do, where evidence is solid for trick-taking games but thin for CCGs, and one competition entry got weaker by spending time on it.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR, CIG 2016; abstract and author blog]** Bursztein: using a dataset of 50,000 Hearthstone replays (May 2014), a model over bigrams/trigrams of card co-occurrence predicts the opponent's most probable future card with accuracy above 95% between turns three and five. — [Bursztein 2016](https://elie.net/publication/i-am-a-legend-hacking-hearthstone-using-statistical-learning-methods)
- **[GREY, author blog]** Earlier figures from the same author: after the early turns the algorithm predicts two cards the opponent has in the deck but has not played with 66.3% success for the first and 44.1% for the second; accuracy rises as the game progresses. — [Bursztein blog](https://elie.net/blog/hearthstone/predicting-hearthstone-opponent-deck-using-machine-learning/)
- **[PR, IPMU 2018; slides read]** Dockhorn, Frick, Akkaya, Kruse: count bigrams of co-occurring cards in a replay database (same turn, successive turns, or both); for each determinization, score candidate cards by co-occurrence with cards already seen, filter by deck-building rules, and sample the opponent's hand in proportion; run an ensemble of MCTS searches over several sampled hands and take a (weighted) majority vote. Reported conclusions: "Guessing a gamestate lets us apply MCTS with weak play strength"; an ensemble over random guesses "helps but does not solve the problem efficient enough"; "an ensemble using such predicted gamestates is nearly as powerful as knowing the real gamestate"; an agent with full information "would yield slightly better results". — [Dockhorn et al. 2018 slides](https://adockhorn.github.io/files/slides/Predicting%20Opponent%20Moves%20for%20Improving%20Hearthstone%20AI.pdf); [paper DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-91476-3_51)
- **[PR]** Santos, Santos & Melo (CIG 2017) handle Hearthstone's hidden information with a database of known decks. — [project page](https://amlsantos.github.io/blog/mcts.html)
- **[PR, AAAI 2019, read in full text]** Solinas, Rebstock, Buro (Skat): a neural network trained on 20 million human games predicts the location of each individual card from bidding and the full card-play history; PIMC then samples worlds in proportion to the product of those probabilities instead of uniformly. Against the state-of-the-art Kermit (which already used count-based inference and was judged comparable to expert humans), the new sampler gained more than 4 tournament points per game in suit and null games (e.g. 21.1 : 17.1 in one null setting; one SD about 1.0–1.4 points), with no significant gain in grand games. PIMC with *no* inference was clearly worst (e.g. 15.6 vs 25.0 points per game against the new sampler in null games). Cost: 0.286 s vs 0.093 s per move at 320 sampled states. — [Solinas, Rebstock, Buro 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09604)
- **[PR]** Kermit's PIMC performance saturated at about 160 sampled states per move (Furtak & Buro 2013, as cited), so beyond that better sampling beat more sampling. — [Solinas, Rebstock, Buro 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09604)
- **[PR]** Negative result in a CCG: ProphetCoac (LOCM) predicted the opponent's hand from draft-phase cards refined by cards already played, to cut branching in minimax; it "did not improve but actually reduced the overall win rate, most likely due to less time available for search". — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PR]** Positive but unquantified: the LOCM agent Chad used "MCTS with the opponent's hand prediction". — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PP]** Privileged opponent information helps a learned policy only modestly: ByteRL's Hearthstone "cheat" variant, which additionally observes up to 30 cards of the opponent's chosen deck (hand still hidden), beat the non-cheating model 55.5% to 44.5%. — [Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05197)
- **[PR]** Upper bound from open-hand play in a classical card game: cheating UCT 56.5% vs determinized UCT 43.6% as Dou Di Zhu Landlord. — [Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75048/1/CowlingPowleyWhitehouse2012.pdf)
- **[PR]** AlphaZe** updates sampling probabilities from information gained during play (e.g. a piece revealed as a bomb) and feeds unknown pieces to the network as probability planes. — [Blüml, Czech, Kersting 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10213697/)

### Inferences
- Ink's bot already knows the opponent's 60-card list, so the "deck archetype prediction" problem that Bursztein and Dockhorn solve is absent. What remains is hand inference: the opponent's hand is not a uniform draw from the unseen cards, because cards they would have played or inked earlier are less likely to still be there. Uniform re-dealing overestimates, for example, cheap playable cards the opponent declined to play.
- The available upper bounds (55.5% for seeing the decklist in ByteRL; "slightly better" for full information in Dockhorn; 56.5 vs 43.6 in Dou Di Zhu) suggest hand inference is a second-order gain, worth a few win-rate points, compared with turn planning and evaluation.
- A cheap version consistent with the evidence and the time budget: weight each sampled opponent hand by a likelihood computed once per decision from the opponent's observed history (cards inked, ink left unspent, cards not played on curve), rather than running inference inside the search loop. This avoids the ProphetCoac failure mode of trading search time for prediction.
- Because the decklist is known and draws are without replacement, exact card counting (removing every revealed, inked-face-up, discarded or played card from the unseen pool) is free and should be verified as the baseline before any learned inference.

### Gaps
- Dockhorn et al.'s numeric win rates and prediction accuracies were not available in the slides; the Dockhorn & Kruse 2020 follow-up ("Predicting Cards Using a Fuzzy Multiset Clustering of Decks") could not be retrieved.
- No study was found that quantifies the gain from *hand* inference given a *known* decklist in a CCG.
- No published opponent-modelling work for Lorcana was found.

## 6. Game-theoretically sound methods (CFR, DeepStack, ReBeL, Student of Games, DeepNash, Pluribus): feasible or relevant?

### Takeaway
Not feasible in their published form for a CCG, and not what CCG practitioners use: public-belief-state search needs to enumerate every private state consistent with public information, which the authors of ReBeL and Student of Games themselves list as the limiting factor, and the equilibrium-seeking agents that do scale to huge hidden-information games (DeepNash, ByteRL) drop search entirely and use industrial-scale model-free self-play. For a hobby project the pragmatic standard is determinized/turn-limited search with a tuned or learned evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR, NeurIPS 2020]** ReBeL's stated limitation: "Most prominently, the input to its value and policy functions currently grows linearly with the number of infostates in a public state. This is intractable in games such as Recon Chess that have strategic depth but very little common knowledge." Its guarantees are limited to two-player zero-sum games; demonstrated on heads-up no-limit hold'em and Liar's Dice. — [Brown et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)
- **[PR, Science Advances 2023; preprint read]** Student of Games' stated limitations: it "currently requires enumerating the information states per public state, which can be prohibitively expensive in some games; this might be approximated by a generative model that samples world states and operates on the sampled subset", it relies on betting abstractions in poker, and "substantial computational resources are used to attain strong play". Demonstrated on chess, Go, hold'em and Scotland Yard. — [Schmid et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.03178)
- **[PR, Science 2022]** DeepNash (Stratego, about 10^535 game-tree states) deliberately uses no search: imperfect-information search requires reasoning over private configurations in a public state and "Stratego computationally challenges all existing search techniques as the search space becomes intractable. We therefore chose an orthogonal route ... without search". It is model-free (R-NaD), with no explicit opponent modelling, and reached 84% wins in 50 ranked games on the Gravon platform (top-3 all-time) and at least 97% against existing bots. — [Perolat et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- **[PP, 2024]** Haluška & Schmid on CCGs specifically: "Agents using online search algorithms enumerate the world state consistent with the current agent's information state. The number of such states in collectible card games is not tractable, and any algorithm that explicitly needs to work with all the states is not feasible." ByteRL, unlike other imperfect-information agents, "does not use search". — [Haluška & Schmid 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16689)
- **[PP]** The game-theoretic motivation still has practical bite: the same paper shows ByteRL's LOCM battle play is "highly exploitable". Behaviour-cloning ByteRL from 125,000 self-play matches (about 3.5M state–action pairs) with a one-hidden-layer network gave 42.4% against it; fine-tuning that clone with PPO as a best-response learner reached 90.4% against ByteRL on a fixed pool of 32 decks, 80.1% on 256, 73.4% on 512 and 54.2% on 1,024 decks. — [Haluška & Schmid 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16689)
- **[PR]** PIMC is exploitable where bluffing/information-hiding is the core of the game (Kuhn poker: loses up to 0.166 per game to a best response), which is why poker moved to CFR on abstractions; in trick-taking games the measured loss to equilibrium was about 0.1 points per game on a [-1, 1] scale. — [Long et al. 2010](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/7562/7562-13-11092-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- **[PR]** ByteDance describes IS-MCTS as game-theoretically "unsafe" yet empirically good in card games. — [Xi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04096)
- **[PR]** Practitioner record: in Hearthstone AI Competition 2018–2020 and LOCM 2018–2021 no winning or listed agent used CFR-family or public-belief-state methods; winners were search plus evaluation, and from 2022 end-to-end RL policies. — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814); [2018](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2018-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf), [2019](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2019-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf), [2020](https://hearthstoneai.github.io/files/slides/2020-Results-Hearthstone-AI-Competition.pdf) slides

### Inferences
- In Lorcana the number of opponent hands consistent with public information is combinatorial (a 7-card hand from about 45 unseen cards of a known list is on the order of 10^7 multisets before weighting), far beyond the roughly 10^3 private states per public state of hold'em that ReBeL/DeepStack-style value networks take as input. Sampling-based approximations are suggested as future work by the Student of Games authors but are not an available, tested method.
- The value of equilibrium play is resistance to exploitation by adaptive opponents. Ink's opponents are human hobbyists and the bot's own earlier versions, not adversarial best-response learners, so exploitability matters less than raw strength and plausible play. Deterministic search bots can be made less predictable cheaply (sampling among near-equal moves).
- The ByteRL exploitability result is a caution for arena evaluation rather than for algorithm choice: a bot tuned only by self-play against itself or one script can look strong and still be beaten by a specialised counter-strategy; evaluating against a pool of diverse opponents and decks is advisable.

### Gaps
- Pluribus, DeepStack and CFR variants were not reviewed from primary sources in this pass; statements about them here are limited to what the cited papers say about the family (public-state enumeration, poker abstractions).
- No attempt to apply CFR/ReBeL-style search to any CCG (even LOCM) was found, so "infeasible" rests on the authors' stated limitations and the Haluška & Schmid argument rather than on a failed experiment.
- No direct practitioner interviews or forum posts by CCG bot authors on this question were collected.

## 7. Work from 2022–2026: which systems search at decision time and which are pure policy networks

### Takeaway
The strongest published CCG agents since 2022 (ByteRL for LOCM and Hearthstone, and by analogy DeepNash) are pure policy networks with no decision-time search, but they were trained on tens of GPUs and thousands of CPU cores for days to weeks and remain exploitable; hobby-scale and open-source projects (ygo-agent, Pokémon TCG PPO agents, MTG Gym benchmarks) also go policy-only but report strength mostly against random or scripted baselines, while shipped game AIs (Forge) and competition entries without big compute remain rule-based or search-plus-evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- **[PP, 2023; COG 2022 winner]** ByteRL for LOCM: one end-to-end policy for deck-building and battle, trained by "optimistic smooth fictitious play" with V-trace; no search at inference. Average win rate against all other submissions 84.41% on LOCM 1.5 and 94.56% on LOCM 1.2. Training: one "unit" = 1 V100 GPU + 600 CPU cores; the LOCM 1.5 submission used 24 units for about 72 hours; the LOCM 1.2 model used 1 unit for 3 days then 8 units for 6 days. — [Xi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04096); corroborated by [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814) ("more than 20% higher win rate than the runner-up" on v1.2)
- **[PR]** At COG 2022 (LOCM 1.5) four of six submissions were neural; two used PPO. No GPU was available at play time. — [Kowalski & Miernik 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11814)
- **[PP → IEEE CoG 2023]** ByteRL for Hearthstone: agents "do not require look-ahead search in both training and inference-time"; one atomic action per network call with an autoregressive (type, target) decomposition and action mask; "end turn" is an ordinary action; LSTM state 256. Main model trained with 24 V100 GPUs and 5,856 CPU cores; the models that played the human were trained 23 and 16 days. Against one streamer (best rank top-10 in the China region, ranked 60–70 at test time) the agents won four Bo5 series 3:0, 3:0, 3:1, 3:2. No comparison with any MCTS agent is reported. — [Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05197)
- **[PP, 2024]** ByteRL is described as state of the art in LOCM and Hearthstone and shown to be highly exploitable in LOCM (figures in section 6). — [Haluška & Schmid 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16689)
- **[GREY, README]** ygo-agent (Yu-Gi-Oh!): RL with an LSTM policy on an envpool/ygopro-core environment, JAX training; no search at decision time ("MCTS Planning" is a roadmap item, as are league training and Nash-equilibrium methods). The reference model was trained on over 100M games, stated as equivalent to 32 RTX 4090 GPUs for 5 days; elsewhere the README gives "8x4090 GPUs and 128-core CPU for a few days" as typical. A 50-iteration single-GPU run reached 98.4% vs random but 3.1% (of 128 games) vs the big model. Demonstrated on a single deck; no human win rates given. — [sbl1996/ygo-agent](https://github.com/sbl1996/ygo-agent)
- **[GREY, model card]** Pokémon TCG (Kaggle "cabt" engine): about 0.5M-parameter pointer policy/value net, PPO self-play with a checkpoint pool; one variant distilled from determinized IS-MCTS (expert iteration); no search at play time; weights are deck-specific; evaluated only against a random agent. — [pkm-cabt-ppo](https://huggingface.co/TomatoCream/pkm-cabt-ppo)
- **[GREY, search snippet]** DeNA presented at CEDEC 2026 on the reinforcement-learning AI for Pokémon TCG Pocket, naming hidden information and probabilistic effects as the main difficulties. — [Inven Global report](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/24133/how-reinforcement-learning-ai-tackles-the-complex-rules-of-pok%C3%A9mon-trading-card-game-pocket)
- **[PP, 2026, search snippet]** MTG-Causal-RL: a Gymnasium MTG benchmark (3,077-dimensional partial observation, 478-action masked space) comparing PPO with a causal variant; both beat random, neither dominates across archetypes. — [Cunha et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.06066)
- **[GREY, project wiki]** MTG Forge's AI "is not 'trained'. It uses basic rules and can be easy to overcome knowing its weaknesses"; it is best with aggro and midrange decks, poor to OK with control and "pretty bad for most combo decks"; logic is heuristics per effect API with some hard-coded single-card logic. — [Forge wiki: AI](https://github.com/Card-Forge/forge/wiki/ai)
- **[GREY, forum summary]** A separate experimental simulation-based AI was added to Forge around 2015; forum users reported poor decisions with Standard decks and suggested using it for specific decisions only. — [slightlymagic forum](https://www.slightlymagic.net/forum/viewtopic.php?p=172241)
- **[PR, IEEE CoG 2023]** Duelyst II: planning-only agents (own-turn MCTS with action-type factoring and a static evaluator, no learning) consistently beat the shipped rule-based AI. — [McKenney & Ruml 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10465745)
- **[GREY, search snippets]** Other 2025–2026 projects: a Riftbound simulator on OpenSpiel shipping MCTS and ISMCTS agents (README warns higher simulation counts get slow quickly); a 2026 Aalborg thesis noting that in the deck-builder Scripts of Tribute "the strongest bots rely on Monte Carlo Tree Search with hand-crafted evaluation functions". — [alpharune](https://github.com/chorlick/alpharune); [Aalborg thesis](https://projekter.aau.dk/projekter/files/822057972/MasterThesis_LT_02_2026.pdf)
- **[PR]** Earlier search-era reference points for the same games: Zhang & Buro (2017) and Świechowski et al. (2018) used determinized MCTS at 0.5–10 s per move; the latter was about level with regular-rank humans and below Legend-rank players. — [Zhang & Buro 2017](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/cig17-hsai.pdf); [Świechowski et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04794)

### Inferences
- There is a compute threshold between the two paradigms. Policy-only agents that beat strong humans used on the order of 24 data-centre GPUs plus 5,000–14,000 CPU cores for days to weeks; a single PC is two to three orders of magnitude short. Below that threshold every documented strong agent is search with an evaluation function, and small learned components (value net, playout policy) are used to sharpen search rather than replace it.
- Policy-only agents in the grey literature are deck-specific and are reported only against random baselines; none demonstrates strength against a competent search bot at hobby compute. That is weak evidence for attempting a pure-policy Lorcana bot on one PC, and consistent with Zhang & Buro's finding that a supervised policy alone lost heavily to a 3-turn search.
- A realistic staged path that matches the evidence: (1) own-turn plan search with pruning and lethal detection; (2) tuned evaluation (evolutionary or Bayesian optimisation of weights, as in LOCM/HS winners); (3) small value network trained on self-play to replace or augment the evaluation; (4) optional learned playout/prior policy; (5) only then consider belief-weighted determinization.

### Gaps
- No published or open-source search/learning agent for Disney Lorcana was found.
- Nothing was found on decision-time algorithms of commercial AIs for Gwent, Legends of Runeterra or MTG Arena, nor a peer-reviewed Slay the Spire agent comparison; these were named in the brief but not located in this pass.
- XMage's AI design (reported in the community as minimax/simulation based) could not be confirmed from a primary source.
- ByteRL's Hearthstone evaluation involves a single human and four series; no independent replication or rating was found, and the Hearthstone paper reports no total number of training games.
- No 2023–2026 paper was found that pits a ByteRL-class policy against a modern determinized-MCTS agent with a learned evaluation under matched decision time.
